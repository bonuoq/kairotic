What it decides

Four parts over a given bass — soprano, alto, tenor — under the rules of Studies 51–59 and chapter 6's (@kairotic/cac's partimento.mjs, four()); the book's realizations are among the ones counted, outlined.

  • The outer voices are counterpoint; the inner voices fill the chords: they may hold a common tone as long as the chords keep it, and need not turn after a leap. No voice crosses another; soprano and alto, alto and tenor, within an octave; no parallel 5ths or octaves between any two voices.
  • Doublings: the bass as a rule (in a 5/3, a 6/4); in a 6/3 the bass, the 3rd or the 6th; never the leading tone, a dissonance or the 7th of a chord. The deceptive ⑥ doubles its 3rd.
  • The dominant's 7th chord and its family — 7 on ⑤, 6/5 on ⑦, 6/4/3 on ②, 4/2 on ④ — are semi-consonances: they need no preparation, but their 7th is held or comes by step (never by leap), and goes down by step (or is held into the 4th of a 6/4), the leading tone up to the tonic (an inner voice may fall to ⑤). Any other 7th chord (ii6/5 on ④, ii7) prepares its 7th: the same note, in the same voice, in the chord before.
  • The Rule of the Octave in four parts, up and down, major and minor: its chords self-contained, the ♯6/4/3 on the descending ⑥ the dominant's 4/3 of ⑤ — in minor the French augmented 6th, its 6th going up to the octave.
  • The cadences: the perfect one and the tenor, alto and discant cadences (the bass singing another voice's clausula), the four-part Galant cadence, the dominant 7th resolved and deceived.
