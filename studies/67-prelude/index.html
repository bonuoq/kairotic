    About

    Study 66 describes BWV 846 by one figure and the five pitches each bar gives it, and reads the bars it does not cover from their notes (the cadence over the tonic pedal, C7 – F/C – G7 – C). What is left is a progression of five voices: each bar's pitches, low to high, a voice each; a voice that keeps its pitch from one bar to the next holds its note (the G of the dominant pedal is one note across eight bars). A progression of voices is what Study 61 reduces: the same grammar (unsplit, unspread, unsuspend — the proto-voices of Finkensiep and Rohrmeier read backwards), GTTM's metre, the economy of the derivation, a chord a unit, the first and the last chord kept and the one where the bass arrives for the cadence. Its frame: C – Am/C – D7 – Dm/F – G7 – G7 (the pedal) – F/C (the subdominant over the tonic pedal) – C.

    Each level is said in bits with Study 66's coder (bits.mjs, a lexicon for each context, an escape for a word never heard): each chord's length and every voice's step from its pitch before — the surface said exactly as Study 66 says its figure's pitches, so the numbers are the same measure. The frame says the harmony in about half of the surface's bits: as a lossy description, a level is a compression. To come back to the surface exactly, the derivation must be said too, and it costs more than it saves: the five voices already move by small steps, cheap words. The reduction explains the harmony more than it compresses it.

    Read forwards, the grammar composes (#321: one grammar, both ways). From a level, its chords are kept where they are and every bar between them is written again, voice by voice — held, a step, a third — to a major or minor triad, the voices not crossing, the steps already said preferred; then Study 66's figure is put on every chord: a new prelude in Bach's manner, the deeper the level the less of his. Its chords are triads (a seventh is a dissonance the grammar still reads only as an ornament), and its key is C major's scale.
