What it decides

The bass is given — a schema of the book, in a key — and the upper voice is written over it unit by unit (half a bar each: the shaded one strong) under the rules of two-part realization: 3rds and 6ths, 5ths and 8ves on the strong units, or a suspension prepared, held and resolved down by step; passing tones over a held bass; no parallel perfect intervals; in minor ⑥ and ⑦ raised going up and natural going down.

  • The figures decide the voice. With them every schema has one figuring — the book's (two to four in minor, where ⑥ and ⑦ may be raised or not); the other realizations the count finds are the same music an octave away, or the bar divided otherwise. So the count that matters is of figurings: without the figures the rules allow over a thousand for the Fifth Down Fourth Up, millions for the 7–6 bass — readings of the same bass the book does not write.
  • The book's prototypes are realizations of their schemata, and close as the book says: imperfect (3–3), perfect (6–8), compound (7–6–8, 2–3–1) and compound leaping (7–3–8 on ②–⑤–①).
  • The rules are tonal, not of a key: every schema counts the same in C major and E♭ major, in A minor and G minor.
  • Drawn uniformly, a realization leaps: most of the millions do (about a third of the moves are steps). The engine weighs them with the soft rules of Studies 38–42 — the contour after Bach, the motion between the voices — and chooses each note by a softmax of −cost / temperature: half the moves or more are steps.
  • Written by hand, each next note shows its futures (and, in red, its cost): the partimento as improvisation, the schema as the plan.
