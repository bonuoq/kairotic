    About

    Study 47. Canons and rings are one thing seen from two sides: a span of time with heads. The span keeps what is written — circular (a ring: positions wrap) or fixed (a work). A writer writes what arrives where it is, and moving it replaces what it passes (or, with mix, leaves it). A reader sends what it crosses, in the order it meets it, either way; a move larger than its jump is a seek. Where a head is comes from a clock (a start and a speed), a signal (a clock moved by a slow wave), a hand, or, for a reader, from following a writer: first its distance d, then its speed. A writer writes what it listens to: the keys, or what a reader sends — then the reader's notes are written again and come back every d: a round. :rec t and :ovr t send a writer to t, moving on at speed 1; a writer's move larger than its jump is a seek, which replaces nothing. So a canon is a reader 1 s behind; two readers, a canon in three voices; speed 0.5, an augmentation; speed −1, a crab — in chunks on a ring, exact on a fixed span written from both ends, where what you play comes back as a palindrome. The values are not transformed here: that is other boxes' work in a patch.

    • Positions in the span's own units (seconds, beats, x) everywhere, with norm for 0…1 (as reader~ reads a sample now).
    • Two writers over one place: replace (the default) or mix — and for sound, how much (mix x).
