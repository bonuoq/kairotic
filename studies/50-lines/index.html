What it decides

The comes is a reader of the dux at a speed p/q — late, some steps away, upright or upside down. The lines are spelled on the finest lattice both fall on (a unit), and Study 42's rules are asked unit by unit: a note longer than a unit is a note held, the first unit of a bar is strong, a dissonance there must be prepared and resolve down by step, on a weak unit it passes by step.

  • The species are relations of time. A canon, an augmentation, a crab and a table canon count exactly as Studies 41–43 counted them (707134 · 8119 · 6959 · 13212).
  • The order of writing follows from the relation. Each step writes the note that leaves the fewest written notes still bearing on a time not judged: forwards for a canon, from both ends for a crab — and for a crab in augmentation from both ends, meeting off the middle.
  • A diminution needs a coda. A comes faster than the dux that ends with it plays the dux's last two notes against them: a parallel perfect interval. Its last note is free.
  • A crab in hemiola closes only at the unison in D dorian: an octave below, nothing.
  • A hemiola leaps by its nature. Its comes reads the dux against itself a note and a half apart, so the dux must sound with itself: of 7,794 works in 10 notes none has more than 4 steps in its 9 moves, and most have one. Drawn uniformly a canon leaps too, because most works do; the engine weighs the works with the soft rules of Studies 38–42 (the contour after Bach, the motion between the voices) and draws the stepwise ones — but in a hemiola the best it finds is still a line of thirds.
