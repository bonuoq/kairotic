About

Two kinds of reduction describe a piece (#399). The surface one (Study 66) says a bar as a figure — which of its pitches comes when — and its pitches, each voice as its step from the last figure's. The genetic one (Studies 61 and 67) says which notes are the bar's sonority (the triad or seventh covering most of its time) and which are ornaments of them: a chord tone by its move in the sonority from the one before (a broken chord rising is the same word over any chord), an ornament by its step from the note before (a passing note, a neighbour). Both are said with one measure (bits.mjs: a lexicon for each context, an escape for a word never heard), so each bar can be said the cheaper way — and after each bar both ways learn it, as a listener who has heard it could say it to itself either way.

Which way each bar took is a signature. BWV 846 and 999 are figuration over harmony: almost every bar a figure. The cello's preludes are a line that unfolds a harmony with its ornaments: a quarter of their bars are said more briefly genetically, and the mixture says them in fewer bits than either way alone. Every bar is said back both ways and gives its pitches again. The scores are Study 66's (the Mutopia Project's public-domain typesets).
